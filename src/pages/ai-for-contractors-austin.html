The problem isn’t your work. It’s the phone.

Someone with a broken AC in an Austin August isn’t leaving a voicemail and waiting until evening. They’re calling the next company on the list. Most home service businesses don’t have a marketing problem so much as a response problem, and it’s one of the easiest things to fix.

What to automate first

1. Missed calls

When a call goes unanswered, the customer gets a text right away asking what they need and when suits them. By the time you’re back in the truck, you have a summary instead of a mystery voicemail.

2. After-hours requests

An assistant on your website handles the questions you answer every day: service area, whether you handle a certain job, what happens next. Then it collects the details and books a time, or flags a real emergency for you.

3. Estimates

Dictate your notes from the driveway. You get a clean written estimate in your format, ready to check and send before you reach the next job, while you’re still the first quote in their inbox.

4. Follow-up on quotes

Many jobs are lost simply because nobody followed up. A polite check-in goes out a few days after each estimate and stops as soon as the customer answers.

5. Reviews

When a job closes, the customer gets a request for a Google review, and you get a draft reply for each new one. Reviews matter for showing up in local map results, and asking every customer beats remembering when you can.

What it works with

We can usually connect to what you’re running, whether that’s a field service platform, QuickBooks, a shared Google Calendar, or a notebook and a spreadsheet. You don’t need a new system to get started.

A note on texting customers

Federal and Texas rules cover automated calls and marketing texts, and AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under federal law. We set things up with consent in mind, keep opt-outs working, and suggest you check anything unusual with your attorney. There’s more in our guide to Texas AI rules.

Common questions

Will customers know they’re talking to an automated system?

We recommend being upfront about it. People mostly care about getting a quick answer, and it’s easy to make sure they can reach you whenever they want a person.

I’m a one-truck operation. Is this overkill?

Often solo operators gain the most, since nobody else can answer the phone. A simple missed-call setup is a small project and a sensible place to start.

What about real emergencies?

You decide what counts, such as a burst pipe or a gas smell. Those can ring through or alert you right away instead of waiting in a queue.
