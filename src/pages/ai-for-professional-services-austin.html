Start with confidentiality

In your line of work, the first question isn’t what AI can do. It’s where client information goes. A free chatbot and a properly configured business tool can have very different terms about how data is stored and used, and your staff may not know the difference.

So we begin by finding out what people are already using, then choosing tools with clear data terms, limiting access, and writing down the rules. Our AI policy template is a good starting point.

For Texas lawyers

The State Bar of Texas Professional Ethics Committee addressed generative AI in Opinion 705 (February 2025), and the American Bar Association issued Formal Opinion 512 in 2024. Both treat AI as covered by existing duties such as competence and confidentiality. Lawyers need to understand the tools they use and verify what comes out. These opinions are yours to interpret, and we build setups that make following your obligations easier: approved tools, access limits and human review.

We’re technologists, not lawyers or CPAs. We can set up the tools and safeguards, but decisions about your professional obligations stay with you.

Common questions

Should our staff be using ChatGPT with client information?

Not without a decision from the firm first. Check the tool’s plan and data terms, decide what may go in it, and write it down. Staff are often already using these tools, so a clear policy beats pretending otherwise.

Can AI hallucinate citations or figures?

Yes. It can produce details that look right and aren’t. Any fact, figure or citation needs to be verified by a person against the original source, which is why we design review into the process.

We’re a very small firm. Where should we start?

Start with intake or document reminders. Both save time every week and neither involves AI giving advice to anyone.
